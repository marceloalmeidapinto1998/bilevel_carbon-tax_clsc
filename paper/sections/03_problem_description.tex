\section{Problem Description}
\label{sec:problem}

We study the tactical design of a multi-echelon, multi-period \CLSC
under an external carbon-tax policy. The decision problem is
hierarchical: a regulator sets a carbon-tax rate, and a supply chain
operator designs and operates the network in response.
\Cref{sec:prob:overview} describes the physical network and its
admissible flows. \Cref{sec:prob:hierarchy} states the Stackelberg
interaction formalized as a \BLMIP in \Cref{sec:model}.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Network and flows}
\label{sec:prob:overview}

The planning horizon spans $T$ discrete periods. The network has five
node classes: suppliers ($\setS$), factories ($\setF$), warehouses
($\setW$), hub terminals $\setH=\setHa\cup\setHs$ (airports and
seaports), and markets ($\setR$). Markets are fixed; they carry demand
as a parameter and collect end-of-use items. The remaining four classes
are decision nodes.

Factories and warehouses are opened. Each carries a construction cost
and an emission, both proportional to installed floor area, with no
separate fixed term. Suppliers and hubs are selected rather than built.
Selecting a supplier incurs a fixed qualification cost and no emission
of its own; its emissions attach instead to the raw material shipped
from it. Selecting a hub incurs a fixed access cost and no construction
emission, since hubs are outsourced cross-docking points rather than
owned infrastructure \citep{mota2018}.

Three item families circulate. Raw materials ($\setU$) are procured
from suppliers and consumed at factories. Finished products ($\setP$)
are manufactured or remanufactured at factories and delivered to markets
through warehouses, hubs, or direct links. Recoverable items ($\setK$)
are collected at markets and returned to factories for remanufacturing,
closing the loop with a product-specific lag. Forward and reverse flows
share the same transportation infrastructure.

Transportation uses a heterogeneous modal portfolio. Road legs use
company-owned trucks; the model selects among truck types differing in
capacity, fixed cost, trip cost, and emission intensity. Long-haul
inter-hub transfers use outsourced air and sea carriers, each contracted
per period with a capacity limit, a variable kg--km cost, and a handling
cost. Hubs cross-dock without storage: within a period, item inflow is
matched by item outflow.

Each factory operates a catalogue of production ($\setG$) and
remanufacturing ($\setL$) technologies. Each technology has a unit cost,
throughput bounds, an emission factor, and a compatible-product set.
Inventory is held only at warehouses, under opening-conditional capacity
limits. Factories are pure flow-through nodes and hold no inventory;
markets satisfy demand exactly with no carry-over.

\Cref{fig:network} shows the admissible arc families. Solid
arcs are forward flows (direct and warehouse-mediated), dashed arcs are
reverse flows, and dotted arcs are intermodal routes through the hub
layer. The two hub nodes depict the same set $\setH$ in its origin and
destination roles along one intermodal path.

\subsection{Bilevel structure}
\label{sec:prob:hierarchy}

Design and operation are governed by an open-loop Stackelberg game
between the regulator (leader) and the operator (follower). The leader
commits to a carbon-tax rate before the follower acts; the follower
observes the rate and responds. The leader chooses the rate from a
finite, discrete grid. This finiteness makes the decomposition-based
bounding procedure of \Cref{sec:methodology} exact and finite.

The leader's objective is lexicographic: it first minimizes the total
emissions its tax induces, then, among all rates achieving that minimum,
selects the lowest rate. The follower is a single, centrally coordinated
operator that minimizes operating cost plus tax payment. Given the
announced rate, it jointly decides facility opening and installed
capacity, technology installation, transport mode and fleet, forward and
reverse flows, production, remanufacturing, and inventory. Facility
opening, supplier and hub selection, and technology installation are
fixed at the start of the horizon and are not reconfigured; flow,
production, and inventory decisions are period-specific. Demand and
return-rate bounds are deterministic and known over the full horizon,
consistent with the tactical scope. When the follower's optimum is not
unique, the leader evaluates its objective under the optimistic
convention.
