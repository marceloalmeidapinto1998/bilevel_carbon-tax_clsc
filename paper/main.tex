\begin{abstract}
A carbon tax reduces emissions only through the decisions it changes, and an operator that redesigns its supply chain responds in discrete steps. Supply chain studies mostly take the carbon price as given; bilevel tax models mostly treat the response as continuous. We formulate a bilevel program in which a regulator sets a continuous tax and the operator solves a mixed-integer closed-loop supply chain (CLSC) design problem. We prove that an optimal tax lies at the lowest admissible tax or at a switching price, where the operator changes its network, and that the tax bill as a share of cost there is invariant to the scale of the emission factors. Enumerating the switching prices traces the response of emissions to the tax; an adapted bounding procedure still bounds the optimum when the operator's problem resists exact solution. On instances calibrated to an electronics producer, the optimal tax attains most of the abatement any network allows, mainly through remanufacturing and cleaner technology, whereas a fixed tax at the European Union allowance price pays more for the same abatement or abates less.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A regulator who anticipates how an operator redesigns its supply chain can choose the lowest carbon tax that induces the operator's cleanest response; a regulator who does not may overshoot or fall short. Carbon is priced in two ways: through cap-and-trade, where the market sets the price, as in the European Union Emissions Trading System, and through taxes, where the regulator does. In either case the price matters through the decisions it changes: which suppliers the operator buys from, which technologies and facilities it uses and how it moves goods \parencite{palak2014analyzing,marufuzzaman2014environmentally,drake2016technology,waltho2019green}.

In most studies of carbon pricing in supply chain planning and design, the price is a parameter, and the studies describe how the supply chain responds to it \parencite{fahimnia2015tactical,zakeri2015carbon,haddadsisakht2018closed,waltho2019green}. That response is not smooth. \textcite{zakeri2015carbon} observe an erratic, non-linear reduction in emissions, with inflection points, as the price rises, and \textcite{krass2013environmental} show that a firm's technology choice can even reverse as the tax rises. A regulator that wants to set the tax well must therefore anticipate how the supply chain will react, not merely observe it.

Choosing the tax is a leader--follower problem, a bilevel program \parencite{kleinert2021survey}: the regulator sets the tax anticipating the operator's best response. Bilevel models of environmental taxes replace a continuous follower by its optimality conditions \parencite{almutairi2014carbon}, search over the policy on a grid \parencite{bard2000bilevel}, or solve an integer follower by problem-specific branch-and-bound \parencite{liu2023optimal}. A closed-loop supply chain (CLSC) design follower is a mixed-integer linear program (MILP) whose objective contains the product of the tax and the emissions. General exact methods for mixed-integer bilevel programs either require the leader's variables to be integer \parencite{lozano2017value} or treat products of leader and follower variables in general \parencite{mitsos2008global,merkert2022exact}, without exploiting that the tax leaves the follower's feasible set unchanged.

This paper sets a carbon tax for an operator that designs a multi-period, multi-product CLSC with the model of \textcite{mota2018sustainable}. It makes three contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{A bilevel model and its structure.} The regulator minimises the emissions it induces plus a small weight on the tax; the operator minimises its cost plus the tax on its discounted emissions. We prove that an optimal tax exists and lies at the lower end of the tax range or at one of the operator's switching prices, the break points of its value function (Proposition~\ref{prop:structure}), and that scaling all emission factors by a common factor divides every switching price by that factor while the tax bill as a share of cost stays unchanged (Proposition~\ref{prop:scale}).
\item \emph{Two exact methods.} An enumeration of the switching prices (Algorithm~\ref{alg:enum}), exact by Lemma~\ref{lem:enum}, and an adaptation of the deterministic bounding procedure (DBP) of \textcite{soares2021deterministic} to a high-point relaxation in which the tax multiplies a continuous emission aggregate, with valid bounds on that product and a certificate at the solver's tolerances (Proposition~\ref{prop:certificate}). Enumeration returns every switching price when the operator's problem can be solved to optimality; the DBP needs few iterations whatever their number and still bounds the optimum when it cannot.
\item \emph{Policy insights.} On instances calibrated to the case study of \textcite{mota2018sustainable}, the optimal tax attains most of the abatement any network allows, mainly through remanufacturing and cleaner production technology, and a fixed tax at the 2025 EU allowance price either pays more for the same abatement or abates less.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:model} states the model and its structure, Section~\ref{sec:methods} the two methods, Section~\ref{sec:results} the computational study, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes.

\subsection{Related literature}\label{sec:literature}

\paragraph{Carbon pricing and closed-loop supply chains} Carbon caps, offsets, cap-and-trade and taxes reduce supply chain emissions mostly by moving the supply chain to lower-emitting resources \parencite{waltho2019green}. Cap, tax, cap-and-trade and offsets change supplier, transport-mode and facility decisions \parencite{palak2014analyzing,marufuzzaman2014environmentally} and the choice of technology and capacity \parencite{drake2016technology}; \textcite{fahimnia2015tactical} ask which price gives the best environmental return per unit of added cost. CLSC network design models under a carbon tax take the tax as given \parencite{haddadsisakht2018closed,govindan2023location}, and \textcite{yenipazarli2016managing} characterises the taxes under which remanufacturing lowers emissions. \textcite{mota2018sustainable} integrate facility location, supplier selection, technology choice, intermodal transport and remanufacturing in one model, which \textcite{tautenhain2021efficient} solve with a Lagrangian matheuristic.

\paragraph{Bilevel tax models and exact methods} \textcite{labbe1998bilevel} formulate the bilevel taxation problem. Regulators set taxes to induce green technology in stylised models \parencite{krass2013environmental,rustico2022environmental}, and \textcite{caselli2026bilevel} survey bilevel models with a sustainability perspective. When the follower has integer variables, exact methods work with its optimal value function \parencite{kleinert2021survey}: branch-and-cut \parencite{fischetti2017new}, value-function reformulations \parencite{lozano2017value,tavasloglu2019solving}, multi-parametric programming \parencite{avraamidou2019multi}, projection \parencite{merkert2022exact} and bounding procedures that alternate a relaxation with the follower's response \parencite{mitsos2008global,soares2021deterministic,soares2023deterministic}. In the pricing problems of \textcite{soares2021deterministic}, prices multiply binary follower variables and the high-point relaxation is a MILP; here the tax multiplies a continuous aggregate. \section{The bilevel model}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Problem and assumptions}\label{sec:assumptions}

A regulator (the leader) sets a carbon tax rate $\tau \in [\tmin, \tmax]$, in \EUR\ per tonne of CO\textsubscript{2}-equivalent (\CO). An operator (the follower) then designs and plans a CLSC over $|\mathcal{T}|$ periods. Raw materials are bought from suppliers and turned into final products at factories; products flow through warehouses to retailers; a fraction of the units sold returns as recovered products, which are collected and remanufactured at factories. Goods move by truck, and by air or sea between hubs. The operator chooses suppliers, factories and warehouses with their floor areas, production and remanufacturing technologies, a truck fleet, and per period the purchases, production, remanufacturing, inventories and flows. The model rests on six assumptions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(A1)] One operator responds to the tax; demand is given and must be met.
\item[(A2)] The operator minimises its discounted cost plus the tax on its discounted emissions; the regulator counts physical, undiscounted emissions.
\item[(A3)] The tax base comprises the emissions of production, remanufacturing, transport and the construction of facilities, and of purchased raw materials where the data assign them; on the calibrated instances these are zero, so the operator is taxed on its own operations (Section~\ref{sec:instances}).
\item[(A4)] All data are deterministic, and the operator's feasible set does not depend on the tax.
\item[(A5)] Among the operator's optimal responses, the one with the least emissions is taken (the optimistic convention).
\item[(A6)] The regulator minimises its emissions plus a weight $\mu > 0$ on the tax rate, which selects the lowest tax among those that induce the same emissions.
\end{enumerate}
The feasible set follows \textcite{mota2018sustainable}, whose equation numbers we cite, with one departure: an installed remanufacturing technology meets its minimum use only in the periods in which it operates, because no returns arrive before the first return lag. The operator's objective departs from theirs in that it minimises cost plus the tax rather than maximising net present value, and prices only the climate-change category of ReCiPe 2008, the one a carbon tax targets.

\subsection{The operator's problem}\label{sec:follower}

At a tax $\tau$, the operator solves
\begin{equation}\label{eq:follower}
\min_{y \in Y} \; f(\tau, y) = c(y) + \frac{\tau}{1000}\,\beta(y),
\end{equation}
where $c$ is the discounted cost \eqref{eq:cost}, $\beta$ the discounted emissions \eqref{eq:emissions}, both written with the flows \eqref{eq:flows}, and $Y$ the set defined by \eqref{eq:balance-factory}--\eqref{eq:use}. Capital is charged at its rental equivalent, recurring fixed costs over the annuity $\Delta$, and flows at their discounted value; the supplier selection cost $f_s$ is zero on every instance, as in both sources:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cost}
\begin{aligned}
c(y) ={}& \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} f_s\, o_s + \varphi^{f} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{F} \cup \mathcal{W}} c^{a}_i A_i + \varphi^{t} \sum_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \Bigl( \sum_{g} \Phi^{g}_g u^{p}_{fg} + \sum_{l} \Phi^{l}_l u^{r}_{fl} \Bigr) \\
&+ \varphi^{v} \sum_{i,\, m \in \mathcal{M}^T} \Phi^{m} V_{im} + \Delta \sum_{h \in \mathcal{H}} f_h\, o_h \\
&+ \Delta\, \Omega \Bigl[ \sum_{i \in \mathcal{F} \cup \mathcal{W}} \pi_i \bigl( \gamma_i o_i + \gamma^{s}_i A_i \bigr) + \sum_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \pi_f \Bigl( \sum_g \gamma_g u^{p}_{fg} + \sum_l \gamma_l u^{r}_{fl} \Bigr) + \sum_{i,\, m \in \mathcal{M}^T} \pi_i \gamma_m V_{im} \Bigr] \\
&+ \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} d_t \Bigl[ \sum_{p,f,g} c^{p}_g q_{pfgt} + \sum_{p,f,l} c^{r}_l r_{pflt} + \sum_{p,w} h_p I_{pwt} + \sum_{s,u} c^{\mathrm{raw}}_{su} \Xout_{ust} + \sum_{r,k} c^{\mathrm{rec}}_{k} \Xout_{krt} \\
&\qquad + \sum_{n,(i,j),\, m \in \mathcal{M}^A \cup \mathcal{M}^S} c^{s}_m \omega_n \delta_{ij} x_{nijmt} + \sum_{(i,j),\, m \in \mathcal{M}^T} c^{t}_m \lambda_m \delta_{ij} z_{ijmt} \\
&\qquad + \sum_{n,(i,j),m} c^{h}_m \omega_n \bigl( [i \in \mathcal{H}] + [j \in \mathcal{H}] \bigr) x_{nijmt} \Bigr].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The emissions of period $t$, the physical total $E$ and the discounted tax base $\beta$, all in kg \CO, are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:emissions}
\begin{aligned}
E_t(y) ={}& \sigma \sum_{i \in \mathcal{F} \cup \mathcal{W}} e^{o}_i A_i + \sum_{p,f,g} e^{p}_g \omega_p q_{pfgt} + \sum_{p,f,l} e^{r}_l \omega_p r_{pflt} \\
&+ \sum_{s,u} e^{\mathrm{raw}}_{su} \omega_u \Xout_{ust} + \sum_{n,(i,j),m} e^{s}_m \lambda^{e}_m \omega_n \delta_{ij} x_{nijmt}, \\
E(y) ={}& \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} E_t(y), \qquad \beta(y) = \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} d_t\, E_t(y).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Construction emissions are amortised over the facilities' 20-year life, as their capital is. The feasible set $Y$ consists of the following constraints and the domains of Table~\ref{tab:variables}.

\paragraph{Flow balances} Final products at factories, warehouses and retailers, every item at hubs, and recovered products, which pass through warehouses without being stored:
\begin{align}
& \Xin_{pft} + \textstyle\sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}_p} q_{pfgt} + \sum_{l \in \mathcal{L}_p} r_{pflt} = \Xout_{pft} && \forall p, f, t \label{eq:balance-factory}\\
& I_{pwt} = I_{pw,t-1} + \Xin_{pwt} - \Xout_{pwt}, \quad I_{pw0} = I^0_{pw} && \forall p, w, t \label{eq:inventory}\\
& \Xin_{prt} = D_{rpt} && \forall p, r, t \label{eq:demand}\\
& \Xin_{nht} = \Xout_{nht}, \quad \Xin_{kwt} = \Xout_{kwt} && \forall n, h, k, w, t \label{eq:transit}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Supply, bills of materials and returns} A selected supplier ships between its minimum order and its capacity \parencite[eqs.~10--11]{mota2018sustainable}; retailers return between a minimum and a maximum fraction of lagged sales, with $D_{rp,t'} = 0$ for $t' \le 0$:
\begin{align}
& \underline{S}_{su}\, o_s \le \Xout_{ust} \le \overline{S}_{su}\, o_s && \forall s, u, t \label{eq:supply}\\
& \textstyle\sum_{p} \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}_p} a_{upg}\, q_{pfgt} = \Xin_{uft} && \forall u, f, t \label{eq:bom}\\
& \textstyle\sum_{p} \eta_{kp}\, \underline{\rho}_{rpt}\, D_{rp,t-\theta_p} \le \Xout_{krt} \le \sum_{p} \eta_{kp}\, \overline{\rho}_{rpt}\, D_{rp,t-\theta_p} && \forall k, r, t \label{eq:returns}\\
& \textstyle\sum_{p} \sum_{l \in \mathcal{L}_p} b_{kp}\, r_{pflt} = \Xin_{kft} && \forall k, f, t \label{eq:rem-bom}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Technologies} A technology is installed only at an open factory, and at most one per product:
\begin{align}
& u^{p}_{fg} \le o_f, \quad u^{r}_{fl} \le o_f, \quad v_{flt} \le u^{r}_{fl} && \forall f, g, l, t \label{eq:tech-open}\\
& \textstyle\sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}_p} u^{p}_{fg} \le 1, \quad \sum_{l \in \mathcal{L}_p} u^{r}_{fl} \le 1 && \forall f, p \label{eq:tech-one}\\
& \underline{Q}_g\, u^{p}_{fg} \le \textstyle\sum_{p \in \mathcal{P}_g} q_{pfgt} \le \overline{Q}_g\, u^{p}_{fg} && \forall f, g, t \label{eq:prod-cap}\\
& \underline{R}_l\, v_{flt} \le \textstyle\sum_{p \in \mathcal{P}_l} r_{pflt} \le \overline{R}_l\, v_{flt} && \forall f, l, t \label{eq:rem-cap}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Facilities} Stock and floor area are bounded at open facilities, and the floor area covers the items entering the facility, at their area per unit over the stock rotation, plus the stock held \parencite[eq.~16]{mota2018sustainable}:
\begin{align}
& \underline{I}_{pw}\, o_w \le I_{pwt} \le \overline{I}_{pw}\, o_w && \forall p, w, t \label{eq:stock}\\
& \underline{A}_i\, o_i \le A_i \le \overline{A}_i\, o_i && \forall i \in \mathcal{F} \cup \mathcal{W} \label{eq:area}\\
& \textstyle\sum_{n} \frac{\alpha_n}{\kappa} \Xin_{nit} + [i \in \mathcal{W}] \sum_{p} \alpha_p I_{pit} \le A_i && \forall i \in \mathcal{F} \cup \mathcal{W}, t \label{eq:area-use}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Transport} A truck mode carries what its integer trips allow, and trips require both endpoints to be open \parencite[eqs.~24--27]{mota2018sustainable}; air and sea carry at most the contracted capacity (eq.~28); goods change between road and air or sea only at hubs of that mode:
\begin{align}
& \underline{X}_m\, z_{ijmt} \le \textstyle\sum_n \omega_n x_{nijmt} \le \overline{X}_m\, z_{ijmt}, \notag\\
& z_{ijmt} \le B_{ijm}\, o_i, \quad z_{ijmt} \le B_{ijm}\, o_j && \forall (i,j),\, m \in \mathcal{M}_{ij} \cap \mathcal{M}^T,\, t \label{eq:trucks}\\
& \textstyle\sum_n \omega_n x_{nijmt} \le \overline{C}_m\, o_i, \quad \sum_n \omega_n x_{nijmt} \le \overline{C}_m\, o_j && \forall (i,j),\, m \in \mathcal{M}_{ij} \setminus \mathcal{M}^T,\, t \label{eq:contracted}\\
& \textstyle\sum_{i \notin \mathcal{H}^{\chi},\, m \notin \mathcal{M}^{\chi}} x_{nihmt} = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{H}^{\chi},\, m \in \mathcal{M}^{\chi}} x_{nhjmt}, \notag\\
& \textstyle\sum_{i \in \mathcal{H}^{\chi},\, m \in \mathcal{M}^{\chi}} x_{nihmt} = \sum_{j \notin \mathcal{H}^{\chi},\, m \notin \mathcal{M}^{\chi}} x_{nhjmt} && \forall \chi \in \{A, S\},\, h \in \mathcal{H}^{\chi},\, n,\, t \label{eq:hubs}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Fleet and use of open nodes} Trucks are based at open nodes that send trips, their driving hours cover the trips, their purchase fits the budget, and every open node carries some flow:
\begin{align}
& V_{im} \le \overline{V}_{im}\, o_i, \quad V_{im} \le \overline{V}_{im} \textstyle\sum_{j, t} z_{ijmt}, \notag\\
& \textstyle\sum_{j} \frac{\lambda_m \delta_{ij}}{\nu_m} z_{ijmt} \le \hat{H}_m V_{im} && \forall i,\, m \in \mathcal{M}^T,\, t \label{eq:fleet}\\
& \textstyle\sum_{i,\, m \in \mathcal{M}^T} \Phi^{m} V_{im} \le \hat{B} \label{eq:budget}\\
& \textstyle\sum_{n,t} \bigl( \Xin_{nit} + \Xout_{nit} \bigr) \ge o_i && \forall i \in \mathcal{O} \label{eq:use}
\end{align}
The bounds $B_{ijm} = \overline{V}_{im} = \lceil \bar{F}/\overline{X}_m \rceil + 1$ use an upper bound $\bar{F}$ on the load of an arc in a period: 7{,}000~t on the generated instances, the generator's largest flow through an entity, and on the case study the most units any arc can carry, a supplier's whole supply or the total production capacity plus the initial stock. A facility that already exists has $o_i = 1$ and its area fixed. In the case study, truck and contracted capacities count units rather than kilograms, handling is charged per unit entering a hub from a non-hub node, and truck emissions count the outbound leg only ($\lambda^e_m = 1$), as in \textcite{mota2018sustainable}; elsewhere $\lambda^e_m = \lambda_m$.

\paragraph{Valid inequalities} Four families tighten the linear relaxation of $Y$ without removing an integer point. An open factory carries flow \eqref{eq:use}, and its inflows are consumed and its outflows made only by its technologies \eqref{eq:balance-factory}, \eqref{eq:bom}, \eqref{eq:rem-bom}, since no final product enters a factory and no raw material or recovered product leaves one; every open factory therefore installs a technology. Recovered products are collected and remanufactured in the same period, so at most
\begin{equation}\label{eq:collectable}
\hat{R}_{pt} = \min_{k:\, b_{kp} > 0} \frac{1}{b_{kp}} \sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}} \sum_{p'} \eta_{kp'}\, \overline{\rho}_{rp't}\, D_{rp',t-\theta_{p'}}
\end{equation}
units of $p$ can be remanufactured in period $t$ ($\hat{R}_{pt} = 0$ if no $k$ makes $p$). Let $\mathcal{R}^{+}$ be the retailers with a positive minimum return in some period and $\mathcal{O}^{T}$ the nodes all of whose outgoing arcs are truck arcs. The inequalities are
\begin{align}
& \textstyle\sum_g u^{p}_{fg} + \sum_l u^{r}_{fl} \ge o_f && \forall f \label{eq:tech}\\
& \textstyle\sum_{p \in \mathcal{P}_l} r_{pflt} \le \min\bigl( \overline{R}_l,\, \sum_{p \in \mathcal{P}_l} \hat{R}_{pt} \bigr)\, v_{flt} && \forall f, l, t \label{eq:vi-rem}\\
& \textstyle\sum_{m} V_{im} \ge 1 && \forall i \in \mathcal{R}^{+} \cap \mathcal{O}^{T} \notag\\
& \textstyle\sum_{m} V_{im} \ge o_i && \forall i \in (\mathcal{S} \cup \mathcal{F}) \cap \mathcal{O}^{T} \notag\\
& \textstyle\sum_{f, m} V_{fm} \ge 1 && \text{if } \mathcal{F} \subseteq \mathcal{O}^{T},\ \textstyle\sum_{r,p,t} D_{rpt} > \sum_{p,w} I^0_{pw} \label{eq:vi-truck}\\
& \textstyle\sum_{f} \sum_{l \in \mathcal{L}_k} v_{flt} \ge 1 && \forall k, t: \textstyle\sum_{r,p} \eta_{kp}\, \underline{\rho}_{rpt}\, D_{rp,t-\theta_p} > 0 \notag\\
& \textstyle\sum_{f} \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}_p} u^{p}_{fg} \ge 1 && \forall p: \textstyle\sum_{r,t} D_{rpt} > \sum_w I^0_{pw} + \sum_t \hat{R}_{pt} \label{eq:vi-tech}
\end{align}
Every subproblem of Section~\ref{sec:methods} includes \eqref{eq:tech}--\eqref{eq:vi-tech}, which use the bound \eqref{eq:collectable}.

\subsection{The regulator's problem}\label{sec:leader}

The regulator anticipates the operator's response \eqref{eq:follower} and solves the optimistic bilevel program
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bilevel}
\min_{\tau,\, y} \; F(\tau, y) = E(y) + \mu\,\tau \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \tmin \le \tau \le \tmax, \quad y \in \arg\min_{y' \in Y} f(\tau, y'). \tag{P1}
\end{equation}
Under (A5), when the follower has several optimal responses the one with the least $E$ is taken, which \eqref{eq:bilevel} expresses by minimising $F$ over $y$ as well. The weight $\mu = \xi\,\Eref$, in kg \CO\ per \EUR/t, states how much emission the regulator gives up to lower the tax by \EUR1/t; $\Eref$ is a lower bound on the emissions of any network (Section~\ref{sec:dbp}), and $\xi = 10^{-4}$ reads ``give up 1\% of the least emissions to avoid \EUR100/t of tax''. As $\xi \to 0$ the problem becomes lexicographic: the least emissions any tax in the range induces, then the lowest tax that induces them. A lower tax for the same abatement limits the transfer from the operator. In computation, the follower's optimality holds only to a tolerance $\varepsilon' > 0$, and the $\varepsilon'$-optimistic problem lets the follower choose any response within $\varepsilon'$ of its optimum \parencite{mitsos2008global}.

\subsection{Structure of the solutions}\label{sec:structure}

The leader's variable appears only in the follower's objective, and the follower's feasible set does not depend on it. The properties of the follower's value function below are classical in parametric integer programming \parencite{geoffrion1977parametric,jenkins1982parametric}; Proposition~\ref{prop:structure} draws their consequences for the leader.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:structure}
Let $Y$ be nonempty and bounded, with integer variables taking finitely many values. Let $\phi(\tau) = \min \{f(\tau, y) : y \in Y\}$, let $\Psi(\tau)$ be its set of minimisers, and let $E^\circ(\tau) = \min \{E(y) : y \in \Psi(\tau)\}$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] $\phi$ is concave, continuous and piecewise linear on $[\tmin, \tmax]$, with finitely many break points $b_1 < \dots < b_m$ in $(\tmin, \tmax)$.
\item[(b)] On each open interval between consecutive points of $\{\tmin, b_1, \dots, b_m, \tmax\}$, $\Psi$ and $E^\circ$ are constant; at each end of such an interval, $\Psi$ contains the interval's set, so $E^\circ$ at a break point is at most its value on either adjacent interval.
\item[(c)] Problem \eqref{eq:bilevel} has an optimal solution, and every optimal tax lies in $\{\tmin, b_1, \dots, b_m\}$.
\item[(d)] For $\varepsilon' > 0$, the $\varepsilon'$-optimistic problem $\min \{E(y) + \mu\,\tau : \tau \in [\tmin, \tmax],\ y \in Y,\ f(\tau, y) \le \phi(\tau) + \varepsilon'\}$ has an optimal solution.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(a) Fixing the integer variables at one of their finitely many values leaves a bounded polyhedron, on which a linear function attains its minimum at one of finitely many vertices; $\phi$ is therefore the minimum of finitely many affine functions $\tau \mapsto c(v) + \tau\,\beta(v)/1000$. (b) Let $J$ be such an interval, on which $\phi$ is affine, and let $y \in \Psi(\tau_0)$ with $\tau_0 \in J$. The affine function $\tau \mapsto f(\tau, y)$ lies above $\phi$ and touches it at the interior point $\tau_0$, so it coincides with $\phi$ on $J$ and, by continuity, at both ends of $J$; hence $y \in \Psi(\tau)$ on the closure of $J$. Each $\Psi(\tau)$ is a finite union of faces of bounded polyhedra, so the minimum that defines $E^\circ$ is attained. (c) On $J$ the leader's objective equals $E^\circ(J) + \mu\,\tau$, which, since $\mu > 0$, exceeds its value at the left end $a$ of $J$, where $E^\circ(a) \le E^\circ(J)$ by (b). The minimum over $[\tmin, \tmax]$ is the least of finitely many values, taken at $\tmin$ or at a break point. (d) The feasible set is nonempty and compact, since $f$ and $\phi$ are continuous and $Y$ is closed and bounded, and the objective is continuous.
\end{proof}

The break points are the operator's \emph{switching prices}: the taxes at which it moves from one network to another. Between two of them a higher tax changes nothing. Their level depends on the operator's emission intensity in a simple way.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:scale}
Multiply every emission factor by $k > 0$, so that $E$ and $\beta$ become $kE$ and $k\beta$ while $c$ is unchanged, and let $\phi_k$ and $\Psi_k$ be the follower's value function and response set after scaling. Then (a) $\Psi_k(\tau) = \Psi(k\tau)$ and $\phi_k(\tau) = \phi(k\tau)$, so every switching price is divided by $k$; (b) at $\tau$ after scaling and $k\tau$ before, each response has the same cost and the same tax bill, so the tax bill as a share of cost is the same; and (c) with $\mu = \xi\,\Eref$, the regulator's problem after scaling is the problem before scaling with weight $\xi/k$ on the range $[k\tmin, k\tmax]$, its optimal tax divided by $k$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(a) and (b): $c(y) + \tau\,k\beta(y)/1000 = f(k\tau, y)$ for every $y \in Y$. (c) $\Eref$, and hence $\mu$, scale by $k$, and $kE(y) + k\mu\,\tau = k\,[E(y) + (\mu/k)\,(k\tau)]$; substituting $\tau' = k\tau$ gives the problem before scaling with weight $\mu/k$ on $\tau' \in [k\tmin, k\tmax]$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:bill}
Let $b$ be a switching price between networks $y^-$ and $y^+$ with $\beta(y^+) < \beta(y^-)$. The tax bill of $y^+$ at $b$ as a share of its cost is $\bigl[(c(y^+) - c(y^-))/c(y^+)\bigr] \big/ \bigl[(\beta(y^-) - \beta(y^+))/\beta(y^+)\bigr]$: the relative cost increase of the switch over its relative reduction of the tax base.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
At $b$ the two responses have equal follower value, $c(y^+) + b\,\beta(y^+)/1000 = c(y^-) + b\,\beta(y^-)/1000$, so $b/1000 = (c(y^+) - c(y^-))/(\beta(y^-) - \beta(y^+))$; multiply by $\beta(y^+)/c(y^+)$.
\end{proof}

The bill share at a switching price is therefore a property of the operator's cost structure, invariant to the scale of the emission factors (Proposition~\ref{prop:scale}(b)). We use it to compare switching prices across operators of different emission intensity and with an observed carbon price.

\section{Solution methods}\label{sec:methods}

Both methods evaluate a tax $\tau_k$ by the same two-stage solve. The follower's problem
\begin{equation}
f^*_k = \min_{y \in Y} f(\tau_k, y) \tag{P3}\label{P3}
\end{equation}
gives its optimal value, and the optimistic response solves
\begin{equation}
\min_{y \in Y} \; E(y) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad f(\tau_k, y) \le f^*_k + \varepsilon' \, |f^*_k|. \tag{P4}\label{P4}
\end{equation}
With $\varepsilon' = 0$, the response $y_k$ of \eqref{P4} is optimal for the follower and, among its optimal responses, has the least emissions, so $(\tau_k, y_k)$ is feasible for \eqref{eq:bilevel}. A tolerance $\varepsilon' > 0$ relative to $f^*_k$ avoids losing feasibility to floating-point arithmetic, as in \textcite{soares2021deterministic}; the problem solved is then the $\varepsilon'$-optimistic one.

\subsection{Enumeration of the switching prices}\label{sec:enumeration}

By Proposition~\ref{prop:structure}(c), the leader evaluates $E^\circ(\tau) + \mu\,\tau$ at $\tmin$ and at the break points of $\phi$ and keeps the best. Each response $y$ defines a line $\tau \mapsto c(y) + \tau\,\beta(y)/1000$, and $\phi$ is their lower envelope. The dichotomic scheme of \textcite{aneja1979bicriteria} finds the break points (Algorithm~\ref{alg:enum}): it evaluates both ends of the tax range, then the tax where the two lines cross. If the response there lies on one of the two lines, the crossing is a break point; otherwise a new line is optimal in between, and the scheme recurses on both halves. The same structure underlies multi-parametric approaches to bilevel MILPs \parencite{avraamidou2019multi}.

\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\caption{Enumeration of the switching prices.}\label{alg:enum}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require tolerances $\varepsilon'$, $\delta_\tau$; weight $\mu$; run budget
\State Evaluate $\tmin$ and $\tmax$ by \eqref{P3}--\eqref{P4}, giving $y_{\min}$ and $y_{\max}$; candidates $\gets \{(\tmin, y_{\min})\}$; pending $\gets \{(\tmin, y_{\min}, \tmax, y_{\max})\}$
\While{pending is not empty and the budget is not spent}
  \State Take $(a, y_a, b, y_b)$ from pending
  \If{$c(y_a) = c(y_b)$ and $\beta(y_a) = \beta(y_b)$, or $b - a \le \delta_\tau$} \textbf{continue} \EndIf
  \State $\tau_\times \gets 1000\,(c(y_b) - c(y_a))/(\beta(y_a) - \beta(y_b))$; evaluate $\tau_\times$, giving $y_\times$
  \If{$y_\times$ lies on the line of $y_a$ or of $y_b$, or its solve stopped on its time limit}
     \State Add $(\tau_\times, y_\times)$ to the candidates \Comment{a switching price}
  \Else
     \State Add $(a, y_a, \tau_\times, y_\times)$ and $(\tau_\times, y_\times, b, y_b)$ to pending
  \EndIf
\EndWhile
\State \Return the candidate with the least $E(y) + \mu\,\tau$ (ties: the lower tax), and all candidates
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:enum}
Let every follower solve be exact with $\varepsilon' = 0$, let $N$ lines be optimal somewhere in $[\tmin, \tmax]$, and let the budget not bind. Then Algorithm~\ref{alg:enum} stops after at most $2N - 1$ evaluations, its candidates are $\tmin$ and every break point of $\phi$, and the candidate it returns is optimal for \eqref{eq:bilevel}.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Two evaluations are made at the ends. Each later evaluation either reveals a line not seen before, at most $N - 2$ times, or certifies that the crossing of two lines adjacent on the envelope is a break point, at most $N - 1$ times. Every break point is the crossing of two adjacent lines, and the recursion reaches every such pair. At each candidate the evaluation returns the optimistic response, hence $E^\circ$, and Proposition~\ref{prop:structure}(c) completes the proof.
\end{proof}

Two responses lie on the same line when their costs and tax bases agree to round-off ($10^{-6}$ relative), not to the MIP gap: lines whose values differ by less than the gap can differ widely in emissions. When a follower solve stops on its time limit, its line is not proven optimal; Algorithm~\ref{alg:enum} keeps the crossing as a candidate without recursing, and the result is not certified. A search stopped by its budget returns the best of every tax evaluated, a feasible point without a lower bound. The candidates and their emissions form the staircase from which a regulator reads what each tax level buys.

\subsection{The deterministic bounding procedure}\label{sec:dbp}

The DBP of \textcite{soares2021deterministic} generates lower and upper bounds on the leader's value until they meet. Like the bounding algorithms of \textcite{mitsos2008global,mitsos2010global}, it alternates a relaxation, tightened by cuts on the follower's value function, with the follower's response at the relaxation's proposal. Our leader minimises, so the relaxation gives the lower bound and the response the upper bound. Dropping the follower's optimality from \eqref{eq:bilevel} gives the high-point relaxation (HPR)
\begin{equation}
\min_{\tau,\, y} \; F(\tau, y) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \tmin \le \tau \le \tmax, \quad y \in Y, \tag{P2}\label{P2}
\end{equation}
and, given the responses $y_1, \dots, y_K$ obtained so far, the HPR with value-function cuts
\begin{equation}
\min_{\tau,\, y} \; F(\tau, y) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \tmin \le \tau \le \tmax, \quad y \in Y, \quad f(\tau, y) \le f(\tau, y_k), \; k = 1, \dots, K. \tag{P5}\label{P5}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:lb}
The optimal value of \eqref{P5} is a lower bound on that of \eqref{eq:bilevel}.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$Y$ does not depend on $\tau$, so every $y_k$ is feasible for the follower at every tax; the follower's optimal value at $\tau$ is at most $f(\tau, y_k)$, and every optimal response satisfies the cuts. \eqref{P5} thus relaxes \eqref{eq:bilevel}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:stop}
Let every subproblem be solved exactly with $\varepsilon' = 0$, and let $(\tau_{K+1}, y')$ solve \eqref{P5} with $K$ cuts. If $\tau_{K+1} = \tau_K$, then $(\tau_K, y_K)$ is optimal for \eqref{eq:bilevel}.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The cut $f(\tau, y) \le f(\tau, y_K)$ makes $y'$ optimal for the follower at $\tau_K$. Its value $F(\tau_K, y')$ is the optimum of a relaxation, hence at most the optimum of \eqref{eq:bilevel}, and at least $F(\tau_K, y_K)$ by \eqref{P4}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:finite}
Let every subproblem be solved exactly with $\varepsilon' = 0$, and let \eqref{P4} return an extreme point of its feasible set. Then the bounds meet after finitely many iterations.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For fixed integer variables, the feasible set of \eqref{P4} is empty or a face of a bounded polyhedron, so the responses belong to a finite set of vertices. If the response $y_{K+1}$ at $\tau_{K+1}$ equals an earlier $y_j$, the point $(\tau_{K+1}, y')$ proposed by \eqref{P5} satisfies $f(\tau_{K+1}, y') \le f(\tau_{K+1}, y_j) = \phi(\tau_{K+1})$, so $y'$ is optimal for the follower and $E(y') \ge E(y_{K+1})$; hence $\mathrm{LB} \ge F(\tau_{K+1}, y') \ge F(\tau_{K+1}, y_{K+1}) \ge \mathrm{UB}$. A new response appears at every iteration until then, which can happen only finitely often.
\end{proof}

Unlike \textcite{soares2021deterministic}, we add no $\varepsilon'$ to the cuts: $y_k$ already lies up to $\varepsilon'$ above the follower's optimum, and a second tolerance would let \eqref{P5} propose a tax at which a lower-emission response lies outside the tie-break set of \eqref{P4}, so the procedure would stall. Since the tax is continuous, \eqref{P5} re-proposes $\tau_K$ only up to the solver's tolerances, and we apply Lemma~\ref{lem:stop} when $|\tau_{K+1} - \tau_K| \le \delta_\tau$. Every subproblem is solved to a relative MIP gap, and the next result states what the bounds then certify.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:certificate}
Let each solve of \eqref{P3} at $\tau_k$ return a value $\bar f_k$ with relative MIP gap at most $\bar g$, so that $\phi(\tau_k) \ge \bar f_k - \bar g\,|\bar f_k|$, and let its response satisfy $f(\tau_k, y_k) \le \bar f_k + \varepsilon'\,|\bar f_k|$. Let LB be a dual bound of \eqref{P5}, $\mathrm{UB} = F(\hat\tau, \hat y)$ the least value evaluated, and $v^*$ the optimal value of \eqref{eq:bilevel}. Then (i) $\mathrm{LB} \le v^*$; (ii) $f(\hat\tau, \hat y) \le \phi(\hat\tau) + (\bar g + \varepsilon')\,|\bar f|$, with $\bar f$ the follower's value at $\hat\tau$; and (iii) if $(\mathrm{UB} - \mathrm{LB})/\max(1, |\mathrm{UB}|) \le \varepsilon$, then $F(\hat\tau, \hat y) \le v^* + \varepsilon \max(1, |\mathrm{UB}|)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(i) Lemma~\ref{lem:lb} uses only $y_k \in Y$, so \eqref{P5} relaxes \eqref{eq:bilevel} whatever the quality of the responses, and a dual bound is at most its optimal value. (ii) Combine the two assumptions at $\hat\tau$. (iii) $\mathrm{UB} \le \mathrm{LB} + \varepsilon \max(1, |\mathrm{UB}|) \le v^* + \varepsilon \max(1, |\mathrm{UB}|)$ by (i).
\end{proof}

The incumbent is thus optimal for the operator to within the MIP gap plus $\varepsilon'$, and its leader value exceeds the exact optimum by at most $\varepsilon$ in relative terms: this is what ``optimal to stated tolerances'' means here. The certificate bounds $F$, not $\tau$. Two taxes on the same plateau of $E^\circ$ differ in $F$ by $\mu\,|\Delta\tau|$, so the stopping test cannot separate taxes closer than $\varepsilon \max(1, |\mathrm{UB}|)/\mu \approx \varepsilon/\xi$, which is \EUR1/t at the settings of Section~\ref{sec:settings}.

\paragraph{The bilinear relaxation} In \textcite{soares2021deterministic} prices multiply binary variables and the product is linearised exactly. Here the tax multiplies the continuous aggregate $\beta(y)$, so \eqref{P2} and \eqref{P5} contain $\tau\,\beta(y)$. We write it as one equality $p = \tau\,\beta$, shared by all cuts, which the solver relaxes by the envelopes of \textcite{mccormick1976computability} inside a spatial branch-and-bound. The envelopes are exact only at the corners of the operands' box, so we add the bounds
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hpr-bounds}
E(y) \ge (1 - \varepsilon')\,\Eref, \qquad d_{\min}\, (1 - \varepsilon')\,\Eref \le \beta(y) \le d_{\max}\, \Ebar, \qquad \tmin\, \beta_{\min} \le p \le \tmax\, \beta_{\max},
\end{equation}
where $\Eref$ is the dual bound of \eqref{P2} solved without the term $\mu\,\tau$, $\Ebar$ the emissions with every facility, technology, supplier and arc at capacity, $d_{\min}$ and $d_{\max}$ the smallest and largest discount factors, and $\beta_{\min}$, $\beta_{\max}$ the bounds on $\beta$. None of them removes a point of the HPR. With $\tmin = 0$, the lower McCormick inequality becomes $p \ge \beta_{\min}\,\tau$ instead of $p \ge 0$.

\paragraph{Subproblems that stop with a MIP gap} On the larger instances, subproblems reach their time limit before their MIP gap target, and we follow \textcite[Section~7]{soares2021deterministic} so that every bound reported remains a bound. LB is the HPR's proven dual bound, valid whatever its gap. If \eqref{P3} stops with value $\bar f$ and gap $g$, the subproblem
\begin{equation}
\min_{y \in Y,\, \zeta \ge 0} \; \zeta \quad \text{s.t.} \quad f(\tau_k, y) \le \bar f\,(1 - g) + \zeta \tag{P6}\label{P6}
\end{equation}
searches for a better response from the incumbent; such a response is not proven optimal, and the upper bound it supports is reported as not certified. The first time \eqref{P5} returns no point within its budget, its cuts become elastic:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\tau,\, y,\, \zeta} \; & F(\tau, y) + \Lambda \textstyle\sum_{k} \zeta_k \\
\text{s.t.} \; & \tmin \le \tau \le \tmax, \; y \in Y, \; f(\tau, y) \le f(\tau, y_k) + \zeta_k, \; \zeta_k \ge 0, \; k = 1, \dots, K,
\end{aligned} \tag{P5-D}\label{P5D}
\end{equation}
with $\Lambda = 10\,\Ebar$ per euro of violation, more than the range of $F$; points with $\zeta = 0$ keep their value, so the lower bound is not weakened. The run also stops after $s_{\max} = 10$ iterations in which neither bound changes, or when its budget is spent.

\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\caption{The DBP of \textcite{soares2021deterministic} for a minimising leader, as implemented.}\label{alg:dbp}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require tolerances $\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon'$, $\delta_\tau$; relative weight $\xi$; stagnation limit $s_{\max}$; run budget
\State Solve \eqref{P2} without $\mu\,\tau$; $\Eref \gets$ its dual bound; $\mu \gets \xi\,\Eref$; add \eqref{eq:hpr-bounds}
\State Solve \eqref{P2}; LB $\gets$ its dual bound; $\tau_1 \gets$ its tax; UB $\gets +\infty$; $k \gets 1$
\Loop
  \State Solve \eqref{P3} at $\tau_k$ (and \eqref{P6} if it stops on its time limit), then \eqref{P4}, giving $y_k$
  \If{$F(\tau_k, y_k) < \mathrm{UB}$} UB $\gets F(\tau_k, y_k)$; incumbent $\gets (\tau_k, y_k)$ \EndIf
  \State Add the cut $f(\tau, y) \le f(\tau, y_k)$ to \eqref{P5} (or \eqref{P5D}) and solve it; $\tau_{k+1} \gets$ its tax; LB $\gets \max(\mathrm{LB}, \text{its dual bound})$
  \If{$(\mathrm{UB} - \mathrm{LB})/\max(1, |\mathrm{UB}|) \le \varepsilon$}
     \State \Return the incumbent: \emph{optimal} if $|\tau_{k+1} - \tau_k| \le \delta_\tau$, no subproblem stopped on its time limit and no slack of \eqref{P5D} is active; \emph{$\varepsilon$-optimal} otherwise
  \EndIf
  \If{neither bound moved for $s_{\max}$ iterations, or the budget is spent} \Return the incumbent and [LB, UB] (\emph{bounds}) \EndIf
  \State $k \gets k + 1$
\EndLoop
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Both classes \emph{optimal} and \emph{$\varepsilon$-optimal} certify Proposition~\ref{prop:certificate}(iii); they differ only in the test of Lemma~\ref{lem:stop}. A result in the class \emph{bounds} is the interval [LB, UB] and its incumbent. Every incumbent is re-evaluated in a fresh model, which must reproduce its feasibility, cost, emissions and leader value.

\paragraph{When each method applies} Enumeration needs no bilinear relaxation, returns every switching price, and costs $2N - 1$ follower solves. The DBP needs a few iterations whatever $N$, each with a non-convex relaxation, bounds the optimum at every iteration even when follower solves cannot be completed, and extends in principle to a vector of taxes, whose linearity regions a one-dimensional search cannot enumerate. Section~\ref{sec:methods-results} compares them; \textcite{merkert2022exact} give a general exact method for products of leader and follower variables, whereas both methods here exploit that the tax leaves $Y$ unchanged.

\section{Computational study}\label{sec:results}

The study answers three questions: how the two methods compare (Section~\ref{sec:methods-results}), where the optimal tax lies and what a fixed tax achieves instead (Section~\ref{sec:anticipation}), and how the tax acts on a real network (Section~\ref{sec:sens-case}).

\subsection{Instances and settings}\label{sec:instances}

\paragraph{Original instances} Fifteen instances follow the generator of \textcite{tautenhain2021efficient}, with parameter ranges from the dataset of \textcite{tautenhain2021data}. Twelve combine 17 or 25 entities, 4 or 8 items and 3, 6 or 12 periods; three larger ones have 12 or 16 items, or 33 entities. Every instance has 3 suppliers, 3 factories, 4 retailers, 3 to 17 warehouses, 2 to 4 airports, 2 seaports, and three production and three remanufacturing technologies. Two departures from the generator matter: transport emission factors are drawn per mode from the case study's values, so that ships, planes and trucks differ, and the annual interest rate of 10\% is compounded over the period length of 17.33 weeks.

\paragraph{Calibrated instances} The generator draws the emission intensity of production, of remanufacturing and of purchased raw materials from one range, the hull of the case study's technology values, caps the return fraction at 15\% and charges a returned unit a fraction of the selling price. On the original instances remanufacturing therefore never exceeds 0.6\% of production. The calibrated instances keep the network, costs, demand and capacities of a generated instance and take from the case study what it determines: production and remanufacturing intensities drawn from its production (375 to 536~kg \CO\ per kg) and remanufacturing (103 to 134) technologies, no emissions on purchased raw materials, a maximum return fraction of 1 and a minimum below 15\%, and \EUR0.15 per returned unit. Two replicates of each of the twelve standard sizes give 24 instances, named \inst{CAL_<size>_r<k>}. They emit 9.5 to 188~kg \CO\ per euro of cost, against 13.5 in the case study; by Proposition~\ref{prop:scale}, this intensity sets the level of the switching prices but not the bill share at which the operator switches.

\paragraph{Case study} The case study of \textcite{mota2018sustainable} is a producer of electronic components in Verona that expands to markets in Europe and Brazil: 3 suppliers, 3 factories and 9 warehouses (one of each already in Verona), 7 markets, 4 airports, 2 seaports, 2 final and 2 recovered products, 4 production and 2 remanufacturing technologies, and 10 yearly periods. We build it from \textcite{mota2018sustainable} and \textcite{tautenhain2021data}, in thousands of units for numerical stability. Its published emission factors put the operator's emissions at about 42~Mt \CO\ without a tax.

\paragraph{Settings}\label{sec:settings} The tax ranges over $[0, 200]$~\EUR/t, about 2.7 times the 2025 EU allowance price of \EUR74/t \parencite{esma2026carbon}, with $\xi = 10^{-4}$. The DBP stops at $\varepsilon = 10^{-4}$, with $\varepsilon' = 10^{-6}$, $\delta_\tau = 0.001$~\EUR/t and $s_{\max} = 10$; enumeration uses the same evaluations. Every subproblem is solved by Gurobi 13.0.1 to a relative MIP gap of $10^{-4}$ (absolute $10^{-10}$) within 600~s, with MIPFocus~2, 4 threads and seed 0; the HPR uses NonConvex~2. Each run has a budget of 1~h. All runs use Python 3.14.4 on a laptop with an Intel Core i7-1185G7 and 15.7~GB of RAM, one at a time.

Figure~\ref{fig:staircase} shows the emission reduction each tax induces on two calibrated instances. Emissions fall in steps: between two switching prices a higher tax changes nothing. On \inst{CAL_I25_M4_T6_r0} the optimal tax, \EUR39.2/t, already reaches the last large step, and \EUR74/t buys the same reduction at a higher bill. \paragraph{The optimal tax captures most of the attainable abatement} Operators have a median of 11 switching prices, from 1 to 28, and the optimal tax ranges from \EUR0 to \EUR196.2/t. The reduction at $\tau^*$ lies within 0.4 percentage points of the bound at $\Eref$ on 10 instances and within 2.7 points on all: the steps beyond $\tau^*$ abate little for a large increase in tax.

\paragraph{A fixed tax overpays or falls short} On the seven instances whose optimal tax lies at or below \EUR74/t, the fixed tax achieves the same reduction, to within 0.09 percentage points, with a bill up to 3.95 times larger. On eight of the nine whose optimal tax lies above, it abates less, by 0.6 to 16.7 percentage points. Only a regulator who knows the switching prices knows which case applies.

\paragraph{Switches are costly relative to the emissions they avoid} At $\tau^*$, the tax bill is 1.4 to 6.2 times the operator's cost. By Corollary~\ref{cor:bill}, the last switch raises the operator's cost by a larger fraction than it lowers its taxed emissions. This share does not depend on the scale of the emission factors, and it makes switching prices comparable across operators of different emission intensity.

\paragraph{The mechanism} The tax acts through the closed loop and production technology. Remanufacturing rises from at most 5.2\% of production without a tax to 6.0--18.1\% at $\tau^*$ on the 14 instances whose returns arrive within the horizon; the production technology changes on 10 of the 16 instances and the factories on 3, while transport stays below 2\% of emissions. This agrees with \textcite{yenipazarli2016managing}, who characterises the taxes under which remanufacturing lowers emissions.

\subsection{The case study}\label{sec:sens-case}

On the case study, the DBP's incumbent cuts emissions by 23.4\%, and the bounds place the reduction at the optimum between 22.6\% and 25.0\%; the tax itself is not determined within the gap. The operator installs the lower-emission production technology for both products and remanufactures 16.0\% of production, against 4.2\% without a tax, as on the calibrated instances. Across six parameters at two levels each (Table~S3 of the Supplementary Material), the network at the incumbent is robust: the same two factories and production technologies in every scenario. The cost of the cleaner technology moves the network without a tax, not the one the tax induces: with that cost halved, at its base value and doubled, the incumbents emit 31.95, 31.87 and 31.88~Mt \CO, while the networks without a tax emit 34.8, 41.6 and 45.9~Mt. A cheaper clean technology is adopted without a tax and leaves the tax less to do.

\subsection{Robustness}\label{sec:robustness}

On the small original instances, re-solving at a MIP gap of $10^{-6}$ returns the same tax to within \EUR0.05/t in 51 of 52 runs, and five solver seeds return the same tax in 19 of 20 runs; the two exceptions change the tax where the operator is indifferent between two networks at a switching price, with emissions within 0.1 percentage points. A 3~h run of the case study changes its incumbent's emissions by 0.07\%. Section~S3 of the Supplementary Material gives the details, and Section~S1 the policy and sensitivity analyses on the original instances.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

A regulator who anticipates how an operator redesigns its closed-loop supply chain needs to consider only the lower end of the tax range and the switching prices. The operator's value function is piecewise linear in the tax, so an optimal tax lies at one of these points, and between two switching prices a higher tax changes nothing (Proposition~\ref{prop:structure}). Each switching price is the cost increase of a switch divided by the taxed emissions it avoids, so the tax bill as a share of cost there is the relative cost increase over the relative reduction of the tax base (Corollary~\ref{cor:bill}); a common scaling of all emission factors divides the switching prices by that factor but leaves this share unchanged (Proposition~\ref{prop:scale}).

Which method to use depends on whether the operator's problem solves to optimality. When it does, enumeration is exact, needs at most $2N-1$ solves for $N$ optimal networks and returns every switching price; it was faster on the four small original instances, with three to six. The deterministic bounding procedure needs few iterations whatever their number, and was faster on five of the eight small calibrated instances, with up to 28. When it does not, as in the case study, only the bounding procedure returns valid bounds.

On the 16 calibrated instances solved exactly, the optimal tax comes within 0.4 percentage points of the bound on attainable abatement on ten and within 2.7 points on all, mainly through remanufacturing, which rises on 14 from at most 5.2\% to 6.0--18.1\% of production, and through production technology, which changes on ten. A tax fixed at the 2025 EU allowance price of \EUR74/t achieves the same reduction with a bill up to 3.95 times larger where the optimal tax lies below it, and abates 0.6 to 16.7 points less on eight of the nine where it lies above. In the case study, the clean technology's cost changes the network without a tax, not the one the tax induces. For a regulator, what matters is where a tax falls relative to the switching prices, which the cost and emissions of the operator's alternatives determine.

These results rest on deterministic data, one case study and emission intensities calibrated to it rather than observed; the tax is set for a single operator; enumeration needs exact solves of the operator's problem; and the case-study bounds leave a gap of 1.2\%, within which the tax is not determined. Extensions include a uniform tax for heterogeneous operators, a welfare-maximising regulator \parencite{krass2013environmental,rustico2022environmental}, competition among operators \parencite{krass2025competitive}, and uncertain demand, returns and carbon prices \parencite{haddadsisakht2018closed,beck2023survey}. The bounding procedure extends in principle to a vector of taxes, where enumeration does not apply, and multiparametric disaggregation \parencite{kolodziej2013global} could speed up its relaxation.
