\section{The original instances}\label{sup:original}

The tables below are those of the first version of this study, on the instances of the original generator, in which the tax base includes the emissions of purchased raw materials (Section~\ref{sec:instances}).

\subsection{A fixed tax on the original instances}\label{sup:anticipation}

A fixed tax that does not anticipate the operator buys the same abatement at a higher bill when the switching price lies below it, and much less abatement when the switching price lies above it (Table~\ref{sup:policy}). Of the eleven instances that close, nine have $\tau^*$ below \EUR74/t. On those nine, the fixed tax achieves the same reduction, to within 0.07 percentage points, with a tax bill 1.3 to 73 times larger. On the other two, \inst{STD_I25_M4_T6} ($\tau^* = 102.3$~\EUR/t) and \inst{STD_I33_M8_T6} ($\tau^* = 129.4$~\EUR/t), it cuts emissions by 11.2\% instead of 28.6\% and by 0.05\% instead of 1.55\%. For each instance we compare the operator's response without a tax, at the regulator's optimal tax $\tau^*$, and at a fixed tax of \EUR74/t, the annual average price of EU emission allowances in 2025 \parencite{esma2026carbon}. An allowance price is a market price, not a tax; we use its level as the benchmark for a tax that does not anticipate the operator's response. The last column of Table~\ref{sup:policy} gives the reduction at $\Eref$, the dual bound on the least emissions of any network: an upper bound on what any tax, or a regulator controlling every decision, could achieve.

Three findings follow. First, a tax at the switching price captures most of the abatement any network allows: the reduction at $\tau^*$ lies within 0.3 percentage points of the bound at $\Eref$ on five of the eleven instances, and within 2.3 points on all. Second, a tax above the switching price buys essentially no further reduction and raises the transfer from the operator to the regulator. When the network is the same, the ratio of the two tax bills is simply $74/\tau^*$. It reaches 73 on \inst{STD_I17_M4_T12}, where \EUR1.0/t secures the reduction for which a regulator at \EUR74/t would collect \EUR3.89~million. Third, where $\tau^*$ lies below \EUR74/t, the cost increase borne by the operator, which excludes the tax, is nearly the same at both taxes, within 0.4 percentage points (1.3--88.9\% on the nine generated instances, 1.3--1.4\% on the case study), because the network is nearly the same.

Where $\tau^*$ exceeds \EUR74/t, the fixed tax stops short of the network the regulator wants. On \inst{STD_I25_M4_T6}, \EUR74/t only makes the operator change its production technology, at a cost increase of 1.3\%; at $\tau^*$ it opens a second factory and installs another production technology at both, which accounts for 86\% of the reduction, at a cost increase of 124\%. On \inst{STD_I33_M8_T6} the response barely moves at \EUR74/t, while $\tau^*$ buys a reduction of 1.55\% at a cost increase of 23.9\%. In both cases any tax below $\tau^*$ induces strictly more emissions, by at least $\mu\,(\tau^* - \tau)$, because $\tau^*$ minimises $E + \mu\,\tau$. A fixed tax at the EU allowance price therefore overshoots or undershoots the operator's switching price, and only a regulator who anticipates the operator's response knows which. On the generated instances the comparison concerns the position of the switching price, not the level of the EU allowance price: at \EUR74/t their tax bill would exceed the operator's whole cost by a factor of 1.1 to 9.3.

On the case study, the fixed tax and the incumbent of the 1~h run give the same reduction, 23.4\%, against at most 25.9\% at $\Eref$. With $\xi = 10^{-5}$, whose run ends at a gap of 0.17\%, the bounds put the reduction at the optimum between 23.3\% and 23.6\%. The remaining points to $\Eref$ therefore need a network that no tax in $[0, 200]$~\EUR/t induces.

These patterns agree with prior studies of carbon pricing. Emissions fall in steps rather than smoothly, like the inflection points that \textcite{zakeri2015carbon} observe in supply chain planning. And an emissions price ranks first among the six climate policies that \textcite{fischer2008environmental} compare for cost-effectiveness. What the bilevel view adds is the location of the step: knowing it, a regulator can set the lowest tax that induces it.

\subsection{Sensitivity analysis on the original small instances}\label{sup:sens-small}

The regulator's weight $\xi$ is the only parameter that moves the optimal tax by more than \EUR3/t, while the cost of the cleanest production technology and the production emission intensity are the parameters that move the emission reduction. We vary six parameters one at a time, each to a low and a high level, on the four small instances (Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}), and solve each of the 48 variants with the DBP; all close their gap.

Scaling every emission factor by the same factor $k$ is not among the scenarios: by Proposition~\ref{prop:scale} it divides every switching price by $k$ and acts on the regulator's choice as the weight $\xi/k$, which the scenarios vary directly.

\paragraph{On most generated instances, the tax acts mainly through the choice of suppliers} We compare each network without a tax with the network at $\tau^*$ on the eleven closed instances. On nine of them, raw-material emissions fall by 2.7 to 97.3~kt \CO\ and account for 55--100\% of the reduction. On the other two, \inst{STD_I25_M4_T6} and \inst{STD_I25_M8_T12}, a change of production technology accounts for 86\% and 85\% of it. On the four small instances, the production technology changes on two (\inst{STD_I17_M4_T3} and \inst{STD_I17_M4_T6}) and the factory opened on two (\inst{STD_I17_M4_T3} and \inst{STD_I25_M4_T3}). Every network computed at \EUR0 and at \EUR74/t ships by truck only: transport makes up 0.1--0.6\% of the emissions, and the tax induces no modal shift. This agrees with \textcite{waltho2019green}, who find that carbon policies cut emissions mostly by moving the supply chain to lower-emitting resources, and with \textcite{palak2014analyzing}, where carbon regulation changes supplier selection. The case study behaves differently (Section~\ref{sec:sens-case}).

\paragraph{The optimal tax is set by the regulator's weight more than by the data} Lowering $\xi$ to $10^{-5}$ raises $\tau^*$ from \EUR16.5 to \EUR32.8/t on \inst{STD_I17_M8_T3} and from \EUR57.5 to \EUR74.6/t on \inst{STD_I25_M4_T3}. Raising $\xi$ to $10^{-3}$ lowers $\tau^*$ to \EUR8.0/t on \inst{STD_I17_M8_T3} and to \EUR23.4/t on \inst{STD_I17_M4_T3}. In every case the emission reduction changes by at most 0.23 percentage points: a regulator who weighs the tax level less buys the next switching price, which abates almost nothing more. The interest rate moves $\tau^*$ by \EUR0.2 to \EUR1.9/t, and the other four parameters by at most \EUR2.4/t (on \inst{STD_I17_M4_T6}).

\paragraph{The cost of clean technology moves the network without a tax, not the network at $\tau^*$} Halving the cost of the cleanest technology lowers the reduction from 52.5\% to 17.6\% on \inst{STD_I17_M4_T3} and from 44.1\% to 20.0\% on \inst{STD_I17_M4_T6}, and doubling it raises the reduction on \inst{STD_I25_M4_T3} from 11.7\% to 27.1\%. These changes come from the network without a tax. On each of the four instances the emissions at $\tau^*$ are the same with the cost halved, at its base value and doubled (24.68, 147.8, 20.52 and 90.12~kt \CO), and so is the network; what changes is whether the operator adopts the clean technology without a tax. Where the reduction stays the same, doubling the cost raises the operator's cost increase, from 9.9\% to 15.6\% on \inst{STD_I17_M4_T3}. Halving and doubling the production intensity moves the reduction on \inst{STD_I17_M8_T3} from 27.2\% to 37.4\% and to 17.5\%, with the same $\tau^*$. The technologies installed at $\tau^*$ are the same in all 12 variants of each instance. \textcite{dong2025technology} reach a related conclusion under cap-and-trade: stricter regulation induces clean technology only if its efficiency exceeds a threshold.

\paragraph{Remanufacturing plays no role in the generated instances} Its share of production is at most 0.6\% in every variant, because remanufacturing emits about as much as production there (Section~\ref{sec:instances}). Its emission intensity and the return fractions change the reduction by at most 0.03 percentage points, and the return fractions only scale the share, from 0.29\% to 0.14\% and 0.58\% on \inst{STD_I17_M4_T3}. The case study, where remanufacturing is 16\% of production at the incumbent, is the test of these two parameters.

\paragraph{The optimal tax of the case study is not determined by these runs} The weight is $\mu = 3.08\cdot10^{6}$~kg per \EUR/t, so the base case's gap of 1.2\% leaves the tax open by about \EUR125/t; the incumbents' taxes are therefore not reported. The emissions are better determined. The reduction at the optimum lies within the bounds of Table~\ref{sup:case}, and the 3~h run of the base case reproduces its emissions within 0.07\% (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}). Five scenarios have bounds disjoint from those of the base case, so their change in the reduction is conclusive: the halved production intensity, the doubled remanufacturing intensity, the halved return fractions and both levels of the clean-technology cost. The bounds of the other scenarios overlap those of the base case, and their differences are inconclusive.

\paragraph{The network is robust} In every scenario the operator opens the same two factories and installs the same production technologies, the lower-emission alternative for each of the two products. The scenarios change volumes and the remanufacturing share, not the factories or the production technologies. The share responds to the return fractions asymmetrically because the case study already lets every unit sold return (a maximum return fraction of 1). Halving the fractions halves that ceiling and the share, from 16.0\% to 8.0\%. Doubling them leaves the ceiling at 1 and only doubles the minimum return, from 15\% to 30\% of sales, which raises the share to 16.8\%. Neither returns nor capacity bind: in the base case the operator collects 78\% of what could return and uses at most 47\% of the remanufacturing capacity in any period, so the volume remanufactured is an economic choice. That remanufacturing responds to the tax only where it carries an environmental advantage is consistent with \textcite{yenipazarli2016managing}, who characterises the taxes under which remanufacturing lowers emissions.


\section{Robustness checks}\label{sup:robustness}

The sensitivity results on the small instances do not depend on the solver's tolerance. Re-solved at a MIP gap of $10^{-6}$ instead of $10^{-4}$, 51 of the 52 runs return the same optimal tax, to within \EUR0.05/t, and the same reduction, to within 0.1 percentage points. The 52 runs are the four base cases and the 48 variants of Section~\ref{sup:sens-small}; each took 8 to 178~s.

The exception is \inst{STD_I17_M8_T3} with twice the production intensity. At the tighter tolerance the run stalls after 15 iterations with its incumbent at \EUR8.77/t, against an optimal \EUR16.48/t at $10^{-4}$. Its lower bound is unchanged (259,574,947 against 259,574,944~kg), and the reduction differs by 0.1 points (17.4\% against 17.5\%). The optimum lies at a switching price, where the operator is indifferent between two networks and the optimistic tie-break decides; which response it selects there depends on the follower's tolerance. This is the tax resolution of Proposition~\ref{prop:certificate} at work: the regulator's emissions are determined, the tax at the break point less so.

The results on the small instances do not depend on the solver's seed either. With seeds 0 to 4, 19 of the 20 runs on the four small instances return the same optimal tax, to within \EUR0.00001/t, the same reduction, the same result class and the same number of iterations; each run took 15 to 48~s. The exception is \inst{STD_I17_M4_T6} with seed 3, which ends $\varepsilon$-optimal after 4 iterations at \EUR7.55/t instead of \EUR7.17/t, with the same reduction. The two taxes lie on the same plateau, \EUR0.39/t apart, within the resolution of Proposition~\ref{prop:certificate}.

Giving the case study 3~h instead of 1~h narrows its gap from 1.2\% to 0.8\% over 10 iterations instead of 3. The incumbent's tax moves by \EUR36/t, while its emissions change by 0.07\% (31.873 against 31.894~Mt \CO) and the operator's cost by 0.05\%. This confirms Section~\ref{sec:sens-case}: on the case study the method determines the emissions and the network, but not the tax that induces them.
